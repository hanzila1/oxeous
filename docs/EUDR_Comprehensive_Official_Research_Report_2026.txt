EXECUTIVE SUMMARY & CURRENT REGULATORY POSTURE (OCTOBER 2026)

•  Paradigm Shift in Global Trade Law: The EUDR transitions European supply chain governance from procedural due diligence (as seen under the predecessor EU Timber Regulation, Regulation (EU) No 995/2010) to a strict market-prohibition regime encompassing seven global forest-risk commodities: cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya, and wood.

•  The Three Pillars of Market Access (Article 3): Relevant commodities and derived products listed in Annex I shall not be placed or made available on the Union market, or exported from it, unless they are: (a) Deforestation-free (post-31 December 2020 cutoff); (b) Produced in accordance with the relevant legislation of the country of production; and (c) Covered by an electronic Due Diligence Statement (DDS) submitted to the TRACES NT Information System.

•  The December 2025 Political Compromise & May 2026 Package: Following intense global diplomatic pressure and industry readiness bottlenecks, co-legislators adopted Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 in December 2025, establishing the binding start date of 30 December 2026 in exchange for major structural simplifications. Crucially, the DDS submission burden is now concentrated on the 'first operator' placing goods on the EU market, while downstream actors pass on reference numbers and Micro/Small Primary Operators (MSPOs) benefit from a one-time simplified declaration.

SECTION 01  |  LEGISLATIVE GENESIS, POLICY ARCHITECTURE & THE 2024–2026 AMENDMENTS

Regulation (EU) 2023/1115 on the making available on the Union market and the export from the Union of certain commodities and products associated with deforestation and forest degradation (EUDR) represents the flagship external supply-chain instrument of the European Green Deal, the EU Biodiversity Strategy for 2030, and the Farm to Fork Strategy. Conceived to eliminate 'embodied deforestation' in European consumption and exports, the regulation was projected by the European Commission's original Impact Assessment (SWD(2021) 326) to prevent at least 71,920 hectares of forest loss annually and reduce global greenhouse gas emissions by at least 31.9 million metric tons of CO2-equivalent per year.

1.1  From FLEGT and EUTR to EUDR: Structural Evolution

Prior to the EUDR, the European Union addressed illicit forestry solely within the timber sector via the 2003 Forest Law Enforcement, Governance and Trade (FLEGT) Action Plan (Regulation (EC) No 2173/2005) and the 2010 EU Timber Regulation (EUTR, Regulation (EU) No 995/2010). However, regulatory fitness checks (REFIT) revealed structural flaws in the EUTR framework:

▪  Legality-Only Scope Limitation: The EUTR only prohibited timber harvested in violation of national laws in the country of harvest. It could not restrict timber resulting from 'legal deforestation'—where producer governments authorized clear-cutting of primary forests for commercial agriculture or timber concessions.

▪  Exclusion of Agricultural Drivers: Scientific assessments demonstrated that agricultural expansion—specifically cattle ranching, oil palm, soy cultivation, cocoa, coffee, and rubber plantations—accounted for nearly 80% of global tropical deforestation, none of which fell under EUTR scrutiny.

▪  Absence of Mandatory Plot-Level Geolocation: Under the EUTR, operators could rely on broad regional origin declarations and paper bills of lading without exact WGS84 spatial coordinates, preventing automated satellite verification by Competent Authorities.

1.3  Deep Dive: Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 & The May 2026 Simplification Package

A critical area where outdated compliance literature fails is the structural reform enacted between December 2025 and September 2026. Under the original 2023 text, every downstream company that transformed an Annex I product into another Annex I product (e.g., a chocolate manufacturer purchasing cocoa butter inside the EU to make chocolate bars, or a furniture maker buying EU-imported sawnwood) was classified as a 'downstream operator' required to file a fresh Due Diligence Statement in TRACES NT.

Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 and the Commission's May 4, 2026 Simplification Package fundamentally overhauled this architecture:

▪  1. Concentration of DDS Filing on the 'First Operator': Since the December 2025 amendment, the full Due Diligence Statement (DDS) is submitted only by the operator that first places the relevant commodity or product on the Union market (or exports it where no prior DDS exists). Downstream operators and traders no longer submit redundant DDS filings in TRACES NT; instead, they collect, verify, and retain the upstream DDS Reference Number and Verification Number.

▪  2. Micro and Small Primary Operators (MSPOs): To prevent disproportionate bureaucratic burdens on European smallholders, forest owners, and micro-farmers in Low-Risk jurisdictions, the amendments introduced a one-time simplified declaration modality in the Information System, avoiding shipment-by-shipment DDS submissions where plot coordinates remain static.

▪  3. Removal of Printed Products (Chapter ex 49): Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 excised Combined Nomenclature heading 'ex 49' (printed books, newspapers, pictures, and other products of the printing industry, manuscripts, typescripts, and plans) from Annex I. While wood pulp and uncoated/coated paper under Chapters 47 and 48 remain strictly in scope, finished printed books and press materials imported into or traded within the EU are permanently exempt.

▪  4. The May 2026 Draft Delegated Act on Product Scope: Following public consultation closing on 1 June 2026 and clarifications in September 2026, the Commission addressed product-scope loopholes and anomalies—proposing the inclusion of soluble/instant coffee and specific palm-oil derivatives (such as soaps containing palm oil derivatives) while clarifying exclusions for waste, second-hand goods, testing samples, retreaded tyres, and standalone transport packaging.

▪  5. Proportionate Legality Evidence & Central Repositories: The updated 2026 Guidance (Section 6) clarifies that 'relevant legislation of the country of production' applies strictly to laws governing the legal status of the area of production, rather than general corporate laws across the entire downstream logistics chain. Furthermore, the Commission launched centralized repositories of national producer-country laws and recognized certification schemes prior to the December 2026 go-live.

CRITICAL LEGAL TRAP: THE EUTR-TO-EUDR TIMBER TRANSITIONAL RULE (ARTICLES 37 & 38)

•  Why Company Size Does Not Delay All Timber Obligations: While Micro and Small Enterprises generally benefit from a deferred application date of 30 June 2027, companies trading in wood and timber products covered by the predecessor EU Timber Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 995/2010) face special transitional rules. Because EUTR was repealed effective 30 December 2025 (with transitional application for timber harvested before that date until 31 December 2028), wood products not covered by the legacy regime transition to EUDR on 30 December 2026.

•  Medium-Sized Enterprises Have No Grace Period: A frequent industry misconception is that all 'SMEs' have until June 2027. Under Article 38(3), only Micro and Small undertakings (as defined in Directive 2013/34/EU) qualify for the 30 June 2027 extension. Medium-sized enterprises must comply alongside Large enterprises on 30 December 2026.

SECTION 02  |  MATERIAL SCOPE: THE SEVEN COMMODITIES & ANNEX I CN/HS RULES

The material scope of the EUDR is governed by a dual-condition trigger under Article 1(1) and Article 2(1)–(2): a product is subject to EUDR obligations if and only if (1) it is listed by its Combined Nomenclature (CN) / Harmonized System (HS) tariff code in Annex I of the Regulation, AND (2) it contains, has been fed with (in the case of live cattle or meat), or has been made using one of the seven 'relevant commodities' (cattle, cocoa, coffee, oil palm, rubber, soya, and wood).

2.2  Crucial Scope Mechanics: The 'ex' Prefix, Recycled Content & Packaging Rules

For customs brokers, ERP architects, and legal compliance teams, three horizontal scope mechanics determine whether an import declaration requires an EUDR Due Diligence Statement reference:

▪  1. The 'ex' Code Rule (Ex-Heading Interpretation): Where a CN code in Annex I is preceded by 'ex' (e.g., ex 9401 for seats, or ex 4011 for tyres), the CN code encompasses broader goods in the EU Combined Nomenclature, but ONLY the subset containing or made from the relevant commodity is regulated. When importing a 100% metal chair under 9401 or a 100% synthetic rubber gasket under 4016, the declarant enters a specific TARIC exemption code on the Single Administrative Document (SAD) declaring the absence of regulated commodities.

▪  2. 100% Recycled / Post-Consumer Waste Exemption (Annex I Note): Goods produced entirely from material that has completed its lifecycle and would otherwise have been discarded as waste (as defined in Article 3(1) of Directive 2008/98/EC) are excluded from EUDR due diligence. However, if virgin wood pulp (even 5% for fiber strength) is blended with 95% recycled paper under Chapter 48, thevirgin fraction triggers full EUDR geolocation and due diligence obligations. Furthermore, industrial sawmill byproducts (sawdust, woodchips, offcuts) never qualify as 'recycled waste' and require full forest-of-origin coordinates.

▪  3. Packaging Material Distinction (Active vs. Passive Packaging): Packing cases, boxes, crates, drums, and pallets (CN 4415) or corrugated cartons (CN 4819) are subject to EUDR when placed on the market as commercial goods (e.g., a packaging manufacturer selling empty cardboard boxes to a logistics firm). Conversely, once packaging is used exclusively to pack, protect, or transport another product—and is not sold as an independent merchandise item—it is exempt from EUDR.

SECTION 03  |  STATUTORY DEFINITIONS, BIOPHYSICAL THRESHOLDS & THE LEGALITY PILLAR

A rigorous scientific and legal understanding of the EUDR hinges on the definitions codified in Article 2 of Regulation (EU) 2023/1115. Unlike voluntary corporate sustainability pledges, EUDR definitions are legally binding and directly parameterize Earth Observation (EO) classification models.

3.1  FAO Forest Thresholds, Deforestation vs. Forest Degradation

The EUDR adopts the quantitative structural parameters of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Global Forest Resources Assessment (FRA):

STATUTORY TERM (ARTICLE 2)

EXACT LEGAL & BIOPHYSICAL DEFINITION

REMOTE SENSING & COMPLIANCE IMPLICATIONS

Forest (Art. 2(4))

Land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 metres and a canopy cover of more than 10%, or trees able to reach those thresholds in situ, excluding land that is predominantly under agricultural or urban land use.

Requires high-resolution satellite canopy height models (CHM, e.g., GEDI LiDAR + Sentinel-2/1 fusion) and minimum mapping units (MMU) of <= 0.5 ha (5,000 m²).

Agricultural Use (Art. 2(5))

Use of land for the purpose of agriculture, including for agricultural plantations (tree stands in agricultural production systems such as fruit tree plantations, oil palm plantations, olive orchards, and agroforestry systems when crops are grown under tree cover) and set-aside agricultural areas, and for rearing livestock.

Crucially classifies oil palm and rubber monocultures as 'agricultural plantations' rather than 'forests'. Thus, clearing natural forest to plant rubber or oil palm constitutes Deforestation.

Deforestation (Art. 2(3))

The conversion of forest to agricultural use, whether human-induced or not.

Applies to all 7 commodities. Note the strict liability phrasing 'whether human-induced or not'—preventing operators from claiming natural wildfire cleared a forest before soy or cattle were introduced post-2020.

Forest Degradation (Art. 2(7))

Structural changes to forest cover, taking the form of the conversion of: (a) primary forests or naturally regenerating forests into plantation forests or into other wooded land; or (b) primary forests into planted forests.

Applies specifically to Wood/Timber commodities (Art. 2(13)(b)). Logging a primary forest after 31 Dec 2020 and replanting it with a monoculture eucalyptus/pine plantation renders the harvested wood non-compliant.

Primary Forest (Art. 2(8))

Naturally regenerated forest of native tree species, where there are no clearly visible indications of human activities and the ecological processes are not significantly disturbed.

Requires historical multi-decade Landsat archive baseline analysis (1984–2020) and disturbance index tracking to distinguish undisturbed primary forest from managed secondary stands.

Plantation Forest (Art. 2(11))

Planted forest that is intensively managed and meets, at planting and stand maturity, all the following criteria: one or two species, even age class, and regular spacing (includes short rotation plantations for wood, fibre, and energy).

Distinguished from broader 'Planted Forest' (Art. 2(10), where >50% of trees are established through planting/seeding). Conversion of naturally regenerating forest to intensive plantation forest post-2020 violates EUDR.

3.2  The Legality Pillar: Eight Mandatory Categories of National Law (Article 2(40))

Even if satellite monitoring confirms with 100% certainty that a plot of land experienced zero deforestation after 31 December 2020, the harvested commodity is still illegal under Article 3(b) if its production violated the 'relevant legislation of the country of production'. Article 2(40) defines this across eight statutory domains concerning the legal status of the area of production:

▪  1. Land Use Rights: Valid land tenure, cadastral registration (e.g., Brazil's CAR - Cadastro Ambiental Rural), concession licenses, and agricultural zoning permits.

▪  2. Environmental Protection: Compliance with national forest codes (e.g., statutory Legal Reserve quotas in the Brazilian Forest Code Law 12.651/2012), protected area buffers, water catchment protections, and Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs).

▪  3. Forest-Related Rules: Forest management plans, harvesting quotas, diameter cutting limits, and biodiversity conservation during logging operations directly related to wood harvesting.

▪  4. Third Parties' Rights: Respect for customary tenure and competing property claims of local communities.

▪  5. Labour Rights: Prohibition of forced labor, worst forms of child labor, and adherence to occupational health and safety standards applicable to agricultural/forestry workers on the production plot.

▪  6. Human Rights Protected under International Law: Protection against violence, land grabbing, or coercion against environmental defenders and rural workers as enshrined in treaties ratified by the producer state.

▪  7. The Principle of Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC): Including as set out in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), where indigenous territories overlap with or are affected by the production plot.

▪  8. Tax, Anti-Corruption, Trade and Customs Regulations: Payment of statutory royalties, stumpage fees, export duties, phytosanitary certificates, and absence of bribery in obtaining land/logging concessions.

SECTION 05  |  THE THREE-STEP DUE DILIGENCE ARCHITECTURE (ARTICLES 8–13)

Prior to placing relevant products on the market or exporting them, upstream operators must execute a structured three-stage Due Diligence framework (Article 8): (1) Information Collection (Article 9), (2) Risk Assessment (Article 10), and (3) Risk Mitigation (Article 11), complemented by an annual public Due Diligence System maintenance and reporting obligation (Article 12).

5.1  Stage 1: Mandatory Information Collection (Article 9)

Regardless of whether a commodity originates in a Low-Risk, Standard-Risk, or High-Risk country, EVERY operator must collect and retain for 5 years the complete Article 9 dataset:

▪  Product & Scientific Identification (Art. 9(1)(a)):  Trade name, type of product, and for products containing or made using wood, the common name of the species AND its full scientific binomial name (e.g., Tectona grandis, Quercus robur). Where multiple species could be present in composite pulp or particle board, the list of all plausible species used must be declared.

▪  Quantity Metrics (Art. 9(1)(b)):  Expressed in net mass (kilograms) and, where applicable in Annex I, in the supplementary unit set out in the Combined Nomenclature (e.g., cubic meters m³ for wood, number of heads for live cattle).

▪  Country of Production & Sub-National Region (Art. 9(1)(c)):  The ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 country code and, where regional risk differentiation applies, the sub-national province or state.

▪  Plot-Level Geolocation & Date/Time Range of Production (Art. 9(1)(d)):  The exact WGS84 latitude and longitude coordinates of ALL plots of land where the relevant commodities were produced, coupled with the date or time range of production (harvesting season or lifespan of cattle).

▪  Supply Chain Actor Counterparties (Art. 9(1)(e)–(f)):  Name, postal address, and email address of any business, operator, or trader who supplied the products, and of any business, operator, or trader to whom the products have been supplied.

▪  Adequately Conclusive & Verifiable Evidence of Zero-Deforestation & Legality (Art. 9(1)(g)–(h)):  Satellite analysis outputs, land title deeds, harvesting permits, tax receipts, FPIC agreements, and audit verifications proving compliance with Article 3(a) and 3(b).

5.2  Stage 2: Risk Assessment Criteria (Article 10)

For products sourced from Standard-Risk or High-Risk countries (or mixed batches containing such origins), the operator must analyze the collected Article 9 data against the fourteen statutory risk assessment criteria in Article 10(2):

▪  (a) Country Risk Tier: The classification assigned to the country of production (or parts thereof) under Article 29 (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093).

▪  (b) Forest Presence & Deforestation Dynamics: The presence of forests and the prevalence of deforestation or forest degradation in the specific country, region, or sub-region of production.

▪  (c) Indigenous Peoples: The presence of indigenous peoples in the country/area of production, Cooperation with them, and the existence of duly reasoned claims regarding the use or ownership of the production plot.

▪  (d) Consultation & Cooperation: Level of consultation and cooperation in good faith with indigenous peoples and local landholders.

▪  (e) Substantiated Concerns & Governance: The prevalence of corruption (e.g., Corruption Perceptions Index, World Bank Governance Indicators), document fraud, lack of law enforcement, human rights violations, or armed conflict.

▪  (f) Supply Chain Complexity & Commingling Risk: The complexity of the relevant supply chain, number of intermediaries/processors, and the risk of circumvention or mixing with products of unknown origin or produced in areas with post-2020 deforestation.

▪  (g) Complementary Certification Schemes: Information supplied by third-party verification schemes (e.g., FSC, PEFC, RSPO, Rainforest Alliance, RTRS, ISCC), provided those schemes meet the robustness criteria in Section 10 of the Commission Guidance (note: certification NEVER substitutes for the operator's legal liability or mandatory plot geolocation).

5.3  Stage 3: Risk Mitigation to 'Negligible Risk' (Article 11)

Under Article 10(1), an operator cannot place a product on the market unless the risk assessment reveals 'no or only a negligible risk' of non-compliance (defined in Article 2(26) as a level of risk where no cause for concern can be discerned after full assessment). If the risk is non-negligible, Article 11 mandates risk mitigation procedures prior to market placement:

▪  Independent Geospatial & Field Audits: Requiring additional high-resolution commercial satellite imagery (e.g., PlanetScope 3m, Pleiades Neo 30cm), UAV orthomosaics, or independent on-the-ground third-party audits.

▪  Scientific Origin Verification Testing: Deploying stable isotope ratio analysis (SIRA), DART-TOFMS (Direct Analysis in Real Time Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry), DNA barcoding, or anatomical wood identification to verify whether physical timber or agricultural commodities match their claimed geographic origin and species.

▪  Supplier Segregation & Capacity Building: Requiring suppliers to physically segregate EUDR-compliant lots from non-compliant domestic trade streams, or delisting non-compliant plots prior to shipment.

5.4  Simplified Due Diligence (Article 13)

When an operator sources relevant commodities exclusively from countries or regions classified as 'Low Risk' under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093, Article 13 permits Simplified Due Diligence. Under this regime, the operator is EXEMPT from executing Stage 2 (Article 10 Risk Assessment) and Stage 3 (Article 11 Risk Mitigation). However, the operator MUST still complete Stage 1 (Article 9 Information Collection, including full plot-level geolocation coordinates), assess the complexity of the supply chain to ensure there is no risk of circumvention or mixing with Standard/High-Risk origins, and submit the DDS in TRACES NT.

SECTION 06  |  TECHNICAL GEOSPATIAL STANDARDS, EO VERIFICATION & TRACES NT ARCHITECTURE

The core innovation of the EUDR is its strict digitization of supply chain provenance via plot-level geospatial coordinates and the European Commission's EUDR Information System (built upon the TRACES NT platform).

6.1  Geolocation Technical Specifications (Article 2(28) & Article 9(1)(d))

Every Due Diligence Statement must contain digital spatial coordinates identifying the exact plot(s) of land (defined in Article 2(27) as land within a single real-estate property sufficiently homogeneous to permit an evaluation of aggregate risk) where the commodity was grown, harvested, or reared:

MANDATORY EUDR SPECIFICATION

IMPLEMENTATION & VALIDATION RULES

Coordinate Reference System (CRS)

WGS 84 (World Geodetic System 1984) — EPSG:4326

Coordinates must be provided in decimal degrees (Latitude, Longitude). Projected coordinate systems (e.g., UTM zones, national grids) are rejected by the TRACES NT validator unless reprojected to EPSG:4326.

For all plots of 4 hectares or more (for non-cattle commodities), a closed polygon using sufficient latitude/longitude vertices to describe the perimeter of the plot is strictly mandatory.

Special Rule for Cattle (All Sizes)

Regardless of whether a cattle ranch spans 2 hectares or 10,000 hectares, establishments where cattle were kept may be described by a single geolocation point per establishment (covering birth farm, grazing pastures, feedlots, and slaughterhouse).

GeoJSON (RFC 7946) FeatureCollection

Polygons must comply with RFC 7946 topological rules: closed linear rings (first and last vertex identical), right-hand rule winding order, zero self-intersections (no 'bowtie' polygons), no overlapping vertices, and no points falling in oceans/waterbodies.

6.2  The Prohibition of Mass Balance vs. Permitted 'Declaration in Excess' (Bag of Plots)

A fundamental compliance boundary in bulk agricultural supply chains (soya, palm oil, cocoa, coffee) is the distinction between prohibited Mass Balance accounting and permitted Identity Preserved / Segregated 'Bag of Plots' aggregation:

▪  Prohibition of Conventional Mass Balance: Under conventional certification 'mass balance' models (e.g., RSPO Mass Balance or Fairtrade Mass Balance), certified compliant volume is mixed in a mill or silo with uncertified/unknown volume, and credits are allocated administratively. Under the EUDR, this is strictly ILLEGAL: if a physical shipment contains even 1% material from an unknown or deforested plot, the entire commingled shipment is non-compliant.

▪  Segregated Aggregation & 'Declaration in Excess' (Commission FAQ Clarification): Where a cooperative or silo physically segregates 100% EUDR-compliant beans from 500 smallholder farms, and a shipment draws from that silo, the operator does not need to know which exact subset of the 500 farms is in a specific shipping container. The operator may declare the geolocations of ALL 500 plots linked to the silo ('declaration in excess'), PROVIDED that (1) every single declared plot is 100% EUDR-compliant, and (2) the physical material is traceable to that finite pool of plots without contamination from unknown sources.

6.3  Earth Observation (EO) & Remote Sensing Verification Pipelines

To verify the December 31, 2020 baseline across millions of polygons, both operators and EU Competent Authorities rely on multi-sensor Earth Observation architectures centered around the EU Copernicus Programme and the Joint Research Centre (JRC):

▪  1. The EU Forest Observatory (JRC Global Forest Cover Map 2020): Developed by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre, the Global Map of Forest Cover for Year 2020 (GFC 2020, updated to V2/V3 at 10m spatial resolution) provides a harmonized global baseline of forest presence as of 31 December 2020. However, the Commission explicitly states that the JRC map is a non-binding screening tool: if a JRC pixel falsely flags a plot due to cloud cover or agroforestry canopy, the operator may override the false positive using higher-resolution time-series optical/SAR imagery or timestamped field truth data.

▪  2. Optical & SAR Sensor Fusion: Because tropical cocoa, oil palm, and rubber zones experience persistent cloud cover, operational EUDR verification pipelines fuse Copernicus Sentinel-2 multispectral time-series (10m resolution, 5-day revisit, analyzing NDVI, NBR, and NDMI indices) with Sentinel-1 C-band Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR backscatter coherence and VV/VH polarization ratios) and ALOS-2 PALSAR L-band radar to detect canopy loss through clouds.

▪  3. Commodity Classification & Canopy Height Discrimination: Distinguishing natural regenerating tropical forest from mature shaded cocoa, agroforestry coffee, rubber monocultures, or oil palm plantations requires integrating spaceborne LiDAR Canopy Height Models (NASA GEDI, ICESat-2) and Vision Transformer (GeoViT) / deep-learning semantic segmentation trained on phenological signatures.

6.4  TRACES NT (EUDR Information System) API & Customs Integration

The EUDR Information System (Article 33), operated on the Commission's TRACES NT platform, serves as the central digital registry connecting Economic Operators, Member State Competent Authorities, and National Customs Authorities via the EU Single Window Environment for Customs (CERTEX):

▪  DDS Submission & Token Generation: When an operator submits a Due Diligence Statement (manually via web GUI or machine-to-machine via SOAP/REST APIs aligned with the May 2026 Implementing Act specifications), TRACES NT assigns a unique alphanumeric DDS Reference Number and a security Verification Number (token).

▪  Customs Pre-Release Check (Article 26): Before goods enter the customs procedure 'release for free circulation' or 'export', the customs declarant enters the DDS Reference Number in Box 44 (or equivalent EU Customs Data Model data element) of the customs declaration. CERTEX automatically queries TRACES NT to verify the status of the DDS. If a Competent Authority has flagged the DDS for suspension (Article 17) or refusal, customs release is automatically blocked at the EU border.

▪  72-Hour Amendment & Withdrawal Window: Submitted DDS filings can be amended or withdrawn by the operator within 72 hours of the reference number being made available in the system, PROVIDED the reference number has not already been used on a customs declaration or referenced by a downstream actor.

SECTION 07  |  COUNTRY BENCHMARKING SYSTEM: IMPLEMENTING REGULATION (EU) 2025/1093

Under Article 29 of the EUDR, all countries initially defaulted to 'Standard Risk' upon entry into force on 29 June 2023. On 22 May 2025, the European Commission adopted Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093 (published in the Official Journal on 23 May 2025), establishing the official three-tier Country Classification List.

7.1  Quantitative & Qualitative Assessment Methodology (Article 29(3)–(4))

The Commission's classification under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093 evaluated every country using FAO Forest Resources Assessment (FRA) data alongside international governance criteria:

▪  Primary Quantitative Criteria (Art. 29(3)): (a) Rate of deforestation and forest degradation; (b) Rate of expansion of agricultural land for the seven relevant commodities; and (c) Production trends of relevant commodities and relevant products.

▪  UN/EU Sanctions Trigger for High Risk: Countries subject to sanctions imposed by the UN Security Council or the Council of the European Union on imports or exports of relevant commodities and products were designated High Risk, as sanctions prevent independent due diligence and verification along value chains.

▪  The 2026 Benchmarking Review: The benchmarking framework is dynamic. A scheduled review draws on the updated FAO Forest Resources Assessment (FRA) dataset published in late 2025. Crucially, the risk tier applicable to a consignment is the tier legally in force at the exact moment the product is placed on the EU market—not the date the commercial purchase contract was signed.

SECTION 08  |  ENFORCEMENT ARCHITECTURE, COMPETENT AUTHORITIES & PENALTIES (ARTICLES 14–25)

Unlike soft-law ESG reporting directives, the EUDR establishes a stringent public enforcement regime executed by designated Member State Competent Authorities (Article 14) operating under harmonized inspection quotas, immediate market-intervention powers, and severe financial penalties.

8.1  Competent Authority Inspection Mechanics & Immediate Interim Measures

Under Articles 16 to 20, Member State Competent Authorities (such as the Federal Office for Agriculture and Food / BLE in Germany, the Federal Forest Office / BFW in Austria, or NVWA in the Netherlands) use a risk-based digital targeting plan fed by TRACES NT alerts:

▪  Automated Geospatial & Algorithmic Checks (Art. 16(3)): TRACES NT automatically cross-references submitted polygon coordinates against satellite deforestation layers, protected area boundaries, indigenous territory maps, and volume plausibility ratios (e.g., flagging a 1-hectare plot claiming to produce 500 metric tons of cocoa).

▪  Immediate Interim Measures / 3-Day Hold (Art. 17): Where a DDS triggers a high-risk alert during customs clearance, the Competent Authority can instruct Customs to suspend release for free circulation or export for an initial 3 working days (extendable by consecutive 3-day periods) to conduct document and physical/laboratory checks.

▪  Mandatory Corrective Action (Art. 24): Where non-compliance is established, authorities require the operator to take corrective action within a specified reasonable period: (a) rectifying formal non-compliance; (b) preventing the product from being placed on the market or exported; (c) withdrawing or recalling the product immediately; or (d) donating the product to charitable/public interest purposes or disposing of it in accordance with EU waste law.

8.2  The Six Statutory Penalties for Infringement (Article 25)

Member States must lay down effective, proportionate, and dissuasive penalties for EUDR infringements, which under Article 25(2) MUST include at least the following six sanctions:

STATUTORY SANCTION (ART. 25(2))

LEGAL & FINANCIAL SCOPE

OPERATIONAL IMPACT ON OFFENDING ENTERPRISE

1. Turnover-Based Administrative Fines (Art. 25(2)(a))

Fines proportionate to environmental damage and value of items; for legal persons, the maximum fine ceiling must be set at AT LEAST 4% of the operator's or trader's total annual Union-wide turnover in the preceding financial year (escalating for repeat offenses).

Creates catastrophic balance-sheet exposure comparable to GDPR infringement penalties.

2. Confiscation of Relevant Products (Art. 25(2)(b))

Mandatory seizure and confiscation of the non-compliant commodities or derived products from the operator and/or trader.

Complete write-off of inventory value at the border or in domestic warehouses, plus storage and destruction costs.

3. Confiscation of Transaction Revenues (Art. 25(2)(c))

Disgorgement and confiscation of all revenues gained by the operator and/or trader from a transaction involving the non-compliant products.

Eliminates any economic incentive to sell non-compliant stock even if physical goods have already reached consumers.

4. Public Procurement & Funding Ban (Art. 25(2)(d))

Temporary exclusion for a maximum period of 12 months from public procurement processes and from access to public funding, including tendering procedures, grants, and concessions.

Disqualifies timber suppliers, food caterers, and tire/paper contractors from government and municipal tenders across the EU.

5. Temporary Market & Export Prohibition (Art. 25(2)(e))

Temporary prohibition from placing or making available on the market or exporting relevant commodities and products in the event of a serious infringement or repeated infringements.

Effectively revokes the company's license to operate in regulated commodity supply chains within the European Union.

6. Loss of Simplified Due Diligence Privilege (Art. 25(2)(f))

Prohibition from exercising the simplified due diligence procedure (Article 13) for Low-Risk countries in the event of a serious or repeated infringement.

Forces the offending company to execute full Article 10 and 11 risk assessments and mitigations even for domestic EU or US imports.

8.3  Substantiated Concerns (Article 31) & Access to Justice (Article 32)

A major enforcement multiplier under the EUDR is the institutionalization of third-party watchdog oversight. Under Article 31, any natural or legal person (including investigative NGOs such as Earthsight, Global Witness, Mighty Earth, or Greenpeace, as well as commercial competitors) may submit 'substantiated concerns' backed by objective evidence (e.g., satellite imagery or supply-chain bills of lading) to Competent Authorities. Competent Authorities are legally obligated to assess those concerns within 30 days and notify the complainant of their actions. Moreover, under Article 25(3), the European Commission publishes a public 'naming and shaming' registry on its website listing legal persons convicted of EUDR infringements, the nature of the violation, and the penalties imposed.

SECTION 09  |  GEOPOLITICAL FRICTION, SMALLHOLDERS & INTERACTION WITH EU ESG LAWS

9.1  WTO Compatibility, Producer-Country Diplomacy & Smallholder Risks

Since its proposal, the EUDR has generated intense trade diplomacy tensions with major agricultural exporting nations (including Indonesia, Malaysia, Brazil, Colombia, Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, and the United States):

▪  WTO Non-Discrimination Defense (GATT Articles I, III, and XX): Producer countries have argued that the EUDR constitutes a unilateral non-tariff barrier to trade. The EU defends the regulation's WTO compatibility on the grounds that it is strictly origin-neutral (applying identically to domestic EU farmers/foresters and non-EU producers under GATT Article III) and justified under GATT Article XX(b) (protection of human, animal, or plant life or health) and Article XX(g) (conservation of exhaustible natural resources).

▪  Smallholder Exclusion Risk & Mitigation Initiatives: Across cocoa (where >80% of West African output comes from 2 million smallholders farming <3 ha), coffee, natural rubber (85% smallholder-grown in Southeast Asia), and oil palm (~40% smallholder share in Indonesia/Malaysia), fragmented cadastres and lack of formal land titles threaten to trigger 'pre-emptive de-risking', where European buyers shift sourcing toward large industrial plantations in Low-Risk or consolidated regions. To counter this, the EU and Member States launched the Team Europe Initiative on Deforestation-free Value Chains (over EUR 86 million in funding, managed alongside the European Forest Institute's Technical Facility) and recognized national traceability platforms (such as Ghana's Cocoa Traceability System, Côte d'Ivoire's Conseil du Café-Cacao national system, and Argentina's VISEC platform for soy/cattle).

9.2  Interoperability with CSDDD, CSRD, and National Supply Chain Laws

Enterprise compliance teams must align EUDR workflows with adjacent European sustainability regulations:

CSDDD — Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (Directive (EU) 2024/1760)

Applies to very large EU and non-EU companies; mandates corporate-wide human rights and environmental due diligence across their 'chain of activities'.

Lex Specialis Relationship: Where EUDR applies to specific commodities and plot-level deforestation/legality checks, EUDR's stricter product-specific rules prevail. However, CSDDD extends human rights and broader environmental obligations (water pollution, chemical waste) to the corporate entity.

Furthermore, companies subject to CSRD may integrate their mandatory annual EUDR public report (Art. 12(3)) into their CSRD report.

Prohibits placing or making available on the EU market, or exporting, ANY product made with forced labour.

Complements EUDR's legality pillar (Art. 2(40)(e) labour rights). While EUDR assesses forced labour laws in the country of production for the 7 commodities, the Forced Labour Regulation applies to all goods globally based on ILO indicators.
